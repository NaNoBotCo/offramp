Most off-ramp losses come from four places: sending on the wrong network, getting played
in a P2P trade, trusting a bad card or app, or handing over your keys. Here's each, with
the habit that defuses it.

## 1. Wrong-network loss (the most common, most avoidable)

Crypto sent over the wrong network, or to an address for a different chain, is almost
always gone — no reversal, no support ticket that helps.

:::warn Defuse it every single time
- Match **coin + network + address format** on both ends before sending.
- Send a **small test amount** to any new address, wait for it to land, then send the rest.
- Beware **same-ticker, different-network**: "USDC" on Base ≠ "USDC" on Ethereum. Agree on
  the network in words, not just the coin.
- Copy-paste addresses and **check the first and last 4–5 characters** — clipboard-swapping
  malware silently replaces addresses.
:::

## 2. P2P trade fraud

When you sell crypto peer-to-peer, the scams cluster around **releasing too early.**

- **The fake payment.** A "buyer" sends a screenshot or a reversible/pending transfer, you
  release the crypto from escrow, and the money never truly arrives — or gets clawed back.
  **Only release after the funds are actually, finally in your account.** Not "sent." Landed.
- **Chargeback rails.** Payments that can be reversed later (some card and e-wallet methods)
  are risky for you as the seller. Favor methods that settle with finality.
- **Off-platform "let's just deal direct."** Anyone trying to move you off the exchange's
  escrow is removing your protection. Stay on-platform.
- **Cash-meet risks.** For in-person cash P2P: meet in a **busy public place** (mall, bank
  lobby with cameras), count the cash fully before releasing, and don't flash large amounts
  or agree to isolated locations.

:::tip The one habit that beats P2P fraud
Escrow exists so you never have to trust the stranger — only the platform. **Never release
crypto until the payment has irreversibly cleared on your side.** Every P2P scam is a
variation on rushing this.
:::

Pick high-volume, long-history merchants — details on the [[off-ramp-thailand|off-ramp
Thailand]] page.

## 3. Fake apps, fake cards, and frozen funds

- **Fake wallet/exchange apps** in app stores and phishing sites that clone a real login.
  Download only from the provider's **official link**, check the URL character by character,
  and bookmark the real one so you never search for it under pressure.
- **"Too good" cards and yield.** A card or platform promising outsized rewards or
  guaranteed returns is a red flag — the [[crypto-cards|fees and terms]] should be boring and
  clearly disclosed.
- **Frozen balances.** Custodial platforms can freeze funds during disputes or compliance
  checks. Keep only a spending float on any card or exchange; hold savings in your own
  [[get-paid-in-crypto|wallet]].

## 4. Seed-phrase and key theft

This is the one that empties everything at once.

:::warn Your seed phrase is your money — guard it like it
- **No one** legitimate ever needs your 12–24 word seed phrase. Every request for it is
  theft. Support agents, "wallet validators," airdrop sites — all fake.
- Type it **only** into your own wallet's recovery screen. Never a website, never a form,
  never a DM.
- Store it **offline** — on paper or metal, not in a screenshot, notes app, email, or cloud.
- Consider a **hardware wallet** so your keys never touch an internet-connected device.
:::

## Pig-butchering and romance-investment scams

A longer con worth naming: a friendly stranger (dating app, "wrong number" text, social
media) builds trust over weeks, then introduces a "can't-lose" crypto investment or a
platform where your balance magically grows. You can even "withdraw" small amounts early —
that's the hook. Then the big deposit vanishes. **Rule of thumb: anyone who reaches out to
you and ends up guiding you into crypto is running a script.** Real opportunities don't
arrive by surprise DM.

## Your five-second pre-send checklist

1. Right **coin**? Right **network**? Address format **matches**?
2. **Test amount** sent and confirmed first?
3. Selling P2P — has the payment **irreversibly cleared**, not just "sent"?
4. Is this the **official app/site** (URL checked)?
5. Did anyone ask for my **seed phrase**? (Then it's a scam. Stop.)

## FAQ

### Can I get crypto back if I send it on the wrong network?
Almost never. There's no central authority to reverse a blockchain transfer. Occasionally a
custodial exchange can help if the funds happen to land on an address it controls, but never
count on it — prevention (matching network + a test send) is the only reliable protection.

### How do I not get scammed selling crypto P2P?
Stay inside the platform's escrow and **only release after the payment has finally cleared
in your account** — never on a screenshot or a "pending" transfer. Choose high-volume
merchants with long histories, and for cash trades meet in a busy, camera-covered place.

### Someone from support asked for my seed phrase — is that normal?
No. It's a scam, full stop. No legitimate service ever needs your seed phrase. Stop, don't
type it anywhere, and contact the provider through their official site.

### Is it safe to keep my money on a crypto card or exchange?
Keep only what you plan to spend soon. Custodial balances can be frozen and are a bigger
target — hold savings in your own wallet, ideally a hardware wallet, and treat cards and
exchanges as a spending float.

### A stranger online is helping me invest in crypto — good sign?
It's a warning sign. Unsolicited "mentors," romance contacts, and surprise DMs that steer
you toward a crypto platform are the classic pig-butchering pattern. The early small
"withdrawals" are bait. Walk away.
